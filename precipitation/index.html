How to use
  1. Tap a well to mix one drop of each solution.
  2. Record which pairs form a precipitate and work out the rules.
  3. Use your rules to identify the mystery solution.

Every solution is 0.1 mol L⁻¹. Rows are metal ions and columns are anions. Tap a well to mix one drop of each.

    Questions and notes

    Solubility rules to compare with your results
    • Always soluble: compounds of group 1 metals (Na⁺, K⁺) and ammonium, all nitrates and acetates. Silver acetate is only moderately soluble.
    • Chlorides, bromides and iodides: soluble, except those of Ag⁺ and Pb²⁺. PbCl₂ and PbBr₂ are slightly soluble.
    • Sulfates: soluble, except those of Ba²⁺ and Pb²⁺. CaSO₄ and Ag₂SO₄ are slightly soluble.
    • Carbonates and phosphates: insoluble, except those of group 1 metals and ammonium.
    • Hydroxides: insoluble, except those of group 1 metals and Ba²⁺. Ca(OH)₂ is slightly soluble.

    "Insoluble" really means very slightly soluble. Whether a precipitate appears depends on the concentrations, which is where Ksp comes in.

    Four wells that are not simple precipitations
    • Cu²⁺ with I⁻ and Fe³⁺ with I⁻: redox reactions. The metal ion oxidises iodide to brown iodine.
    • Fe³⁺ with CH₃COO⁻: a complexation reaction. A soluble red-brown complex ion forms, so the colour changes but nothing precipitates.
    • Fe³⁺ with CO₃²⁻: the precipitate is iron(III) hydroxide, not a carbonate, and carbon dioxide is released.

    Leave these wells out when you write your solubility rules. They are still useful for identifying ions.
