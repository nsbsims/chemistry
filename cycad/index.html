Food from a poisonous plant

Cycads are ancient seed plants. In Australia they include species of Cycas, Macrozamia, Lepidozamia and Bowenia. Their large seeds are packed with starch, and for many Aboriginal groups they were an important food. Fresh seeds, however, contain toxins that cause severe illness.

The main toxins are cycasin (in Cycas) and macrozamin (in Macrozamia). Both are molecules with a sugar group attached. The many –OH groups on the sugar form hydrogen bonds with water, which makes these toxins soluble in water. That solubility is the key to removing them.

Methods recorded in published accounts

Beck (1992) grouped the ways Cycas seeds were prepared in northern Australia into three techniques, and found that each was effective at removing cycasin:

  • Brief leaching. Fresh seeds were roasted and cracked, then leached in still or running water for about a day. They had to be eaten soon afterwards.
  • Prolonged leaching. Kernels were dried and pounded without cooking, then left in water for three to seven days. The product kept well.
  • Ageing. Seeds were left under the trees, or dried, stored or buried, for several months.

Asmussen (2011) compared accounts from across the continent. For Macrozamia in eastern New South Wales, historical records from Port Jackson, the Hunter region and Bega describe kernels being soaked in water for periods ranging from a few days to two or three weeks. In some accounts the kernels were first roasted and pounded, or bruised to a pulp, and after soaking they were eaten or roasted in the coals. In south-western Australia, Noongar people ate the outer flesh of Macrozamia seeds after soaking it in water, burying it in sand pits for weeks, or both.

Methods varied between places and peoples, and written records capture only part of this knowledge.

The chemistry of leaching

  • Dissolving. Toxin molecules at the surface of a kernel piece dissolve into the surrounding water.
  • Still water is a closed system for the toxin. As the toxin concentration in the water rises, toxin molecules also move back into the kernel. Eventually the two rates are equal and leaching stops, with toxin still in the food. Changing the water removes the dissolved toxin, so the equilibrium shifts and more dissolves.
  • Running water is an open system. Dissolved toxin is carried away at once, so its concentration in the water stays close to zero and equilibrium is never reached. The toxin keeps dissolving until almost none is left.
  • Surface area. Slicing or pounding exposes far more surface to the water, so the toxin leaves faster. This is collision theory at work.
